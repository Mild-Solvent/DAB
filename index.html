What it does

A private map, and the two ways you actually talk to people on it.

Everything below runs between phones. There is no DAB server to sign into, go down, get breached, or change its mind about your data later.

Fog of war

The world starts covered. Streets you physically walk stay uncovered, in 180 m cells stored on your phone. It turns a map into something you fill in.

Friends, live or last-seen

When you are both online, positions stream phone-to-phone over WebRTC. When they are offline, you still see their last known position — encrypted, waiting on a public relay.

Encrypted chat, photos, files

Messages live on the two phones and nowhere else. Attachments are chunked and pushed down the direct channel, or through expiring encrypted events when it is closed.

Audio and video calls

One-to-one calls signalled over encrypted events and carried by DTLS-SRTP. STUN only — if a direct path cannot be made, DAB tells you instead of quietly routing you through someone else's relay.

Works offline

Download the area you are in and the map keeps rendering with no connectivity at all. Vector tiles come from OpenFreeMap — no API key, no quota, no signup.

Text-and-calls mode

A hard switch, not a checkbox. The map never mounts, GPS is never requested, and no position leaves the phone. Your explored map waits on disk for when you switch back.

How it works

Three moving parts carry your data, and none of them is a company.

DAB has no backend, no TURN service, no push vendor and no analytics. It borrows free public infrastructure that only ever sees ciphertext. The one routine request it makes to a company server is the update check below — it carries nothing about you, and it switches off.

Pair by QR code

Each phone generates a keypair and keeps the secret in the OS keystore. You add a friend by scanning their code, or by sending the pairing link through any messenger you already use.

Relays carry the ciphertext

Location, messages and call setup are published as end-to-end encrypted events to free public Nostr relays. A relay sees an encrypted blob and nothing else — not the content, not who it is for.

Phones talk directly

When both of you are online, a WebRTC data channel opens between the two devices and positions, messages and calls take the direct path instead.

Install & update

Sideload once, then updating is one tap.

DAB is not on the Play Store. Every build is published here as a release, and every release is signed with the same key — so a new APK installs straight over the old one and keeps your friends, chats and explored map.

  1. Download the APK on your phoneTap the download button on this page from the phone itself. Your browser will warn you about the file type — that is normal for anything outside the Play Store.
  2. Allow installs from your browserAndroid asks once, per app. Settings → Apps → your browser → Install unknown apps.
  3. Open the file and installPlay Protect may offer to scan it. Let it, or skip it — either way the install completes.
  4. After that, DAB tells you about updates itselfIt asks GitHub for the latest release at most once a day, while you have the app open, and offers you the download when there is a newer one. Install it straight over the old one — no uninstall, no data loss, no re-pairing. One toggle in Settings → Updates switches the automatic check off, and this page stays the fallback.

Read this before you trust it with anything

DAB is alpha software built for a handful of friends, not an audited product. It has not been through a security review.

The APK is signed with a development key, so your phone will treat it as an app from an unknown developer — that is expected here, and it is exactly why you should only install it if you know where this build came from.

Peers in a call or a direct transfer can see each other's IP address, which is inherent to peer-to-peer and not something the encryption hides.

The update check is the app's one routine request to a company server: it carries no identifiers, but it shows your IP address to GitHub about once a day until you switch it off.

Privacy

What it protects, stated plainly.

The honest version, written from reading the code rather than the intent — the full document is in the repository.

  • Message content, attachments and call media are end-to-end encrypted.
  • Who you talk to is wrapped too — relays cannot read the participants off an event.
  • No account, no email, no phone number, no analytics, no crash reporting.
  • Notifications show a friend's name and "New encrypted message" — never the body.
  • Relays and call peers see your IP address and the timing of your traffic.
  • With no push service, a killed iOS app cannot be woken for an incoming call.
  • The daily update check shows your IP to GitHub, until you switch it off.
